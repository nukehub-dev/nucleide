The activation-to-transport handoff keeps three things separate: the raw
Monte Carlo tally, the physical group flux, and the integrated photon source a
transport code samples. This demo exercises the shared Rust source contract —
the same validator, density conversion and joint-probability kernel the Python
package uses.

## Voxel spectra and units

The mesh is a regular `(2, 2, 1)` grid with four 1 cm³ voxels and four photon
groups spanning 0–0.1, 0.1–0.5, 0.5–2 and 2–10 MeV. The table shows
**integrated photons/s** per group, not a density and not a probability.
Change a value and select **Evaluate source**; the shared validator reports
stable issue codes and keeps modelling assumptions separate from errors.

Negative values, non-finite cells, nonascending edges and mismatched
dimensions raise clear errors. A zero-source voxel contributes no samples.
An all-zero source remains valid data and has no sampling distribution.

## Joint voxel/group sampling

The probability table is the joint distribution over voxel/group pairs. It is
not the product of a spatial marginal and an energy marginal: independent
sampling would invent cross-pairs that carry no source. The declared
assumptions are uniform photon density per eV inside each group and uniform
position inside each voxel.

## Allocate spectra in mixed voxels

In **Mixed-voxel allocation**, zone A spans two 2 cm³ voxels. Zone B also
occupies part of the first voxel. Select **Allocate mixed source** to see
how the supplied intersection volumes distribute each zone's group
strengths. The default overlap volumes send 10 and 30 photons/s from zone A
to the two voxels. Zone B contributes 60 photons/s to the first voxel in a
different energy group.

Edit the declared zone volumes, group strengths or intersection volumes
to try another allocation. The shared kernel checks the mapped volume
of each emitting zone and the total occupancy of each voxel. The result
treats unassigned volume as source-free before distributing the assigned
source uniformly throughout the voxel.

For a transport model, one possible input is OpenMC's material volume per
mesh cell<Citation id="openmc-mesh-material-volumes" />. Supply the mapping
from those material IDs to your activation zones.

## Cooling times

The three buttons are **declared snapshots** at 0 s, 3600 s and 7200 s; the
browser does not compute decay physics. The cooling-selection check calls the
shared exact-match kernel. Requested offsets must match an available offset
exactly, duplicates are rejected, and a near match is not substituted.

Python adds the transport-facing pieces: OpenMC `MeshSource` export, batch
files, and reproducibility manifests. See
[Activation to transport](../python/activation-to-transport.md) for the full
workflow.

The browser snapshots are synthetic because no nuclear data or transport
engine is shipped to the page. The shared kernel is checked against real
OpenMC 0.16 photon and neutron transport with a caller-mounted NNDC HDF5
library in `validation/openmc_transport_probe.py`; that check covers
normalization, source-strength scaling and data loading, not dose or
transport accuracy.

| External check (OpenMC 0.16 + NNDC HDF5) | Photon | Neutron |
| --- | --- | --- |
| strength-10 / strength-1 tally ratio | 9.999999999999988 | 10.000000000000014 |
| per-source vs rate-scaled flux difference | 1.26e-15 | 1.33e-15 |
| water flux, 1/(cm²·s) | 0.027611 | 0.031235 |
| iron flux, 1/(cm²·s) | 0.019920 | 0.041691 |

The runs use 10 cm water/iron spheres, a centered 2 MeV photon or 14 MeV
neutron point source, and 20 000 particles × 5 batches with a fixed seed. The
water and iron fluxes differ because the real cross sections are loaded; the
checks do not establish a dose or transport-accuracy claim.
